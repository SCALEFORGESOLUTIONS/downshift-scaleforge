Executive summary

Here's the short version. We're entering a crowded K-8 market with a mix we didn't find in any competitor we reviewed: a ready-made library of standards-aligned songs across five subjects, practice games that use the song's own audio as the game (like Lyric Lockdown and Rhythm Recall), and real-time analytics for every student on standards-tagged content. A teacher gets the full library, four game modes and basic analytics on a plan that's free forever for classroom use. Each piece exists somewhere else. Flocabulary has songs, practice and dashboards, but it's built around hip-hop and sold mostly to schools and districts. Blooket, Gimkit and Kahoot have games and low teacher prices, but no songs. Suno can make a custom song in minutes for about what Premium costs. And since April 2026, MagicSchool, an AI platform with a free teacher plan, makes fully produced, curriculum-aligned songs with lyrics and voice in under a minute (company claim). So a song made to order is no longer our edge. Our spot is real, but narrow. We win on what happens after the song: games played inside the music, a vetted library, per-student data and K-8 vocabulary, plus accuracy and child safety. No single feature carries us.

Key strategic findings

  1. Custom songs are now a commodity. Suno's free plan gives 50 credits a day, which Suno says is enough for 10 songs, and Pro costs $8 a month billed yearly or $10 monthly. MagicSchool now makes sung, curriculum-aligned songs from a topic and grade level, on a platform with a free teacher plan. Our AI custom jams can't carry the paid plan on their own.
  2. Premium sits at the top of the teacher-plan band. At $7.99 a month ($95.88 a year paid monthly), we're about 60% above the $59.88 annual plans at Gimkit and Baamboozle. Our annual option, $6.99 a month billed annually ($83.88 a year, labelled "Save 12%"), is still about 40% above them. Quizlet Plus also charges $7.99 billed monthly. The open question: should annual move closer to that anchor?
  3. Google Classroom sync is expected, not special. Kahoot's Google Classroom add-on posts assignments and sends scores back to the Classroom gradebook. So Classroom sync can't be the main reason a teacher pays for Premium.
  4. Districts keep or cut tools on cost, ease of use and teacher buy-in. Federal ESSER relief money has run out, and analysts expect vendor contracts to be among the first cuts. A low-cost school license backed by usage data is our most realistic first school sale.
  5. Compliance comes before the sale. Districts expect the amended COPPA rule, state student-privacy laws, signed data privacy agreements and accessibility rules (including captions for every song) to be covered before they sign.
  6. The research on songs is promising, but conditional. We don't publish any product-specific efficacy data yet, so an ESSA Tier IV logic model is the right first step.

What we'll do next, at a glance

In order: finish the compliance and accessibility kit before district outreach. Line up our site's pages before launch. Test moving the annual price closer to the $59.88 anchor, while keeping the paid value in advanced analytics, the full game set, offline downloads and school features, not in custom jams or Classroom sync. Call the product "retrieval practice set to music", keep evidence claims honest, and use our 1,801-term K-8 glossary to show what each jam covers. Post free sample songs on short-form video. Build a guard-railed custom jam feature that students under 13 can use safely. And offer a modest school license plus a pilot study that could lead to ESSA Tier III evidence. Where a figure is a company's own claim, or a price isn't publicly listed, we say so.

Three kinds of rivals. None does it all.

We face three kinds of rivals, and each one pressures a different part of our offer. Educational music, where Flocabulary is the only scaled player with practice and dashboards. Game-based practice, a crowded field of free-to-start quiz tools. And AI song tools, which let any teacher turn a lesson into a song in minutes, now including teacher platforms like MagicSchool.

Flocabulary is the big music rival, and it sells mostly to districts.

Flocabulary has made educational hip-hop since 2004. Nearpod bought it in April 2019 (EdSurge). In February 2021, Renaissance agreed to buy Nearpod, Flocabulary included (Business Wire, company release). EdSurge reported the price at about $650 million in cash; the companies didn't disclose terms (EdSurge, 2021). Each lesson runs through a video, Vocab Cards, a Vocab Game, Break it Down, Read & Respond, a Quiz and Lyric Lab, where students write rhymes over a beat they choose (Flocabulary, How it works; Flocabulary blog). The Plus plan adds student accounts, assignments, dashboards, practice sets and LTI integration with Google Classroom, Clever and ClassLink (Flocabulary FAQ, May 2024).

Flocabulary still offers a teacher "Lite" plan with a 30-day free trial, but its pricing page returned an error on October 10, 2026, and the Lite price is not publicly listed (Flocabulary FAQ). School deals are better documented. Lincolnwood School District 74 in Illinois was quoted $3,120 for grades 3 to 5 and negotiated it to $2,800 for 2022-23 (board packet, August 2022). Its 2023-24 renewal for the same grades was quoted at $3,360 (board packet, August 2023). Utah Education Network's FY26 price list gives Flocabulary Plus at $2 per student a year, or $5 per student in a bundle with Nearpod EL and Nearpod 21st Century (UEN). Reach figures vary. Flocabulary said in 2015 that over 35,000 schools used it (School Library Journal), and its 2024 FAQ says teachers at 20,000 schools use its videos (Flocabulary FAQ). Both are company claims, and they may measure different things.

Two things matter for us. Flocabulary's library is big: 1,300+ hip-hop videos and lesson activities (Flocabulary blog, July 2024), sitting next to 200+ Nearpod Originals. It's also growing in Spanish, with Flocabulary Español math lessons since March 2026 (Flocabulary blog, March 2026). And the individual teacher route still exists, so we shouldn't claim Flocabulary has no teacher plan.

The rest of the music field is free, passive or narrow.

Outside Flocabulary, the music-for-learning products we compete with split two ways: free sponsor-funded platforms and small niche subscriptions. GoNoodle made its paid teacher tier free for all educators from the 2021-22 school year, and it says sponsorships keep it free for teachers, families and schools (Tech & Learning, 2022; GoNoodle partnerships FAQ). It focuses on movement, brain breaks and mindfulness, with some curricular songs and short question sets. We found no standards tagging or per-student analytics. NUMBEROCK sells animated math songs with worksheets and auto-graded assessments for $4.95 a month after a 30-day free trial, $29.88 a year (the annual plan starts with "180 Days Free") or $149.95 for a "Lifetime" plan, covering roughly kindergarten to grade 6 math (NUMBEROCK). HeidiSongs targets preschool to grade 2 and special-needs learners, with more than 1,500 videos (its page says both 1,500 and 1,600) and plans from $0.99 a month up to $9.99 a month for All Access (HeidiSongs.tv). Learning Upgrade is closest to us in kind: songs, games and progress reporting, now aimed at K-12 intervention and adult education such as ESL, GED and workforce programs (Learning Upgrade). It doesn't publish school prices (quotes on request) and offers a 3-month free pilot (Learning Upgrade, quote page). It also sells a consumer iPhone app, with a $4.99 monthly subscription and 1-, 3- and 12-month licenses at $7.99, $19.99 and $59.99, rated 3.2 from 24 ratings (App Store). A 2014 review listed $30 per student, $7,500 per school and 20 free student licenses (Tech & Learning, 2014).

Many teachers also use free YouTube channels. On their official channel pages in October 2026, Jack Hartmann had about 5.66 million subscribers (YouTube), Scratch Garden about 2.3 million (YouTube) and KLT (formerly Kids Learning Tube) about 2.74 million (YouTube). They're videos, not a classroom product, but they set what teachers expect to pay for a song: often nothing.

Game platforms are cheap, familiar and quick to add AI.

Teachers know this group well. Kahoot's plans page lists paid teacher tiers at $3, $7, $12 and $19 a month billed annually, or $9, $15, $25 and $34.99 billed monthly (Kahoot plans). The same page's FAQ says education plans bill annually only. For schools, Kahoot publishes per-host prices: EDU Standard at $14.99 a host a month ($179.88 a year) and EDU Pro at $24.99 ($299 a year), both billed annually, with district plans by quote (Kahoot EDU). Its Google Classroom add-on lets teachers post kahoots as Classroom assignments, and grade passback sends scores to the Classroom gradebook automatically. A school admin installs the add-on, and a Google subscription may be required (Kahoot Help). A group led by Goldman Sachs Asset Management took Kahoot private for about $1.7 billion. The deal was announced in July 2023 (K-12 Dive) and closed with Kahoot's delisting on January 23, 2024 (Kahoot release). Kahoot also owns Clever, the rostering service many US schools use, which it bought in 2021 for up to $500 million (TechCrunch). Gimkit Pro costs $59.88 a year or $14.99 a month, and Gimkit's group plans cost $650 a year for a department (up to 20 teachers) and $1,000 a year for a school (Gimkit Help). Blooket's Plus price is not publicly listed: it shows only inside the signed-in upgrade flow, and group plans are by quote (Blooket Help, individual; Blooket Help, group). Quizizz renamed itself Wayground on June 24, 2025, says it's used in 90% of U.S. schools (company claim) and now pitches curriculum alignment and a "Flexible AI Generation Hub" (Quizizz Inc. via PR Newswire). Quizlet Plus costs $35.99 a year or $7.99 billed monthly, the same monthly price as our Premium, and Plus Unlimited costs $44.99 a year or $9.99 monthly (Quizlet). Quizlet pitches Plus to students ("Get better grades"), but it's a well-known price for a monthly study subscription. Prodigy is free to teachers and earns money from parent memberships (Prodigy). Legends of Learning offers more than 2,000 curriculum-aligned math, science and history games, mainly for K-8 (Legends of Learning). Its free Basic Teacher plan is limited to 3 active assignments and limited gameplay. Student, class and standard-level reporting comes with Premium Classroom at $790 per teacher a year and School at $4,450 for 400 to 1,000 students (Legends of Learning pricing). On analytics, it's the closest to us in this group.

AI question generation is now standard here. Kahoot includes it from its $3 Bronze tier (Kahoot plans). Gimkit launched an AI Question Generator in September 2025 (Gimkit on X). Blooket has offered AI question sets through Khan Academy's Khanmigo since January 2025 (Khan Academy Blog; Blooket Help). Music in these products is background only. Kahoot offers a set of built-in background tracks with volume control, and hosts can't upload their own audio (Kahoot plans). On Gimkit, audio questions need a paid membership (Gimkit Help). Our quiz-board and competition games, like Quiz Show, Streak Sprint and Gold Rush, overlap with these tools. They're table stakes, not reasons to switch. The five games that use song audio, like Lyric Lockdown and Rhythm Recall, are the real difference.

AI song tools made custom songs cheap. Now teacher platforms make them too.

This group is the newest, and it moves fastest. Suno launched its v6 models on September 9, 2026, with a free v6-mini model for everyone, built with licensing partners Warner Music Group, BMG and Believe (Music Business Worldwide). Suno's own pricing page lists a Free plan with 50 credits a day, which Suno says is "enough to make 10 songs per day", with no downloads and no commercial use. Pro is $8 a month billed yearly or $10 monthly, with 2,500 credits and 20 song downloads a month. Premier is $24 a month billed yearly or $30 monthly, with 10,000 credits and 60 downloads (Suno pricing; Suno Help). Teachers already use Suno. Edutopia shows class-written songs on topics like triangle types (Edutopia), and Israel's Ministry of Education and Singapore's Centre for Chinese Language list Suno teaching practices and training (Israel Ministry of Education; SCCL). Some teachers write lyrics in a teacher tool such as MagicSchool or Knowt, then turn them into songs in Suno (MagicSchool; Knowt).

These tools have clear limits for K-8. Suno's Terms bar users under 13 and allow ages 13 to 17 only with a parent's or guardian's prior consent (Suno Terms, effective September 3, 2026). Music in Gemini launched with Lyria 3 (30-second tracks) for users 18 and older in February 2026 (Google). Lyria 3 Pro, with tracks up to three minutes and still 18+, reached personal accounts and Education editions in April 2026 (Google Workspace Updates). Lyria 3.5 followed in September 2026, and its announcement states no age rule (Google). So most K-8 students can't use these tools directly. General music generators such as Suno and Gemini don't align output to standards, and teachers are advised to check AI lyrics for accuracy before sharing them (TeachersFirst). Legal risk remains, too. Universal and Sony sued Suno a second time on September 18, 2026, in federal court in Boston, calling v6 "the fruit of the same poisoned tree" (Music Business Worldwide). Udio turned off downloads on October 30, 2025, after settling with Universal, and Warner settled with Udio on November 19, 2025 (AP via WSLS; Music Business Worldwide). Udio still sells plans: Free (10 credits a day and 100 a month, with up to 3 full-length songs a day), Standard at $10 a month or $96 a year, and Pro at $30 a month or $288 a year (Udio pricing).

The bigger change is that teacher platforms now make the songs themselves. In April 2026, MagicSchool launched an Educational Song Generator. A teacher picks a topic, grade level and musical style, and it makes "fully produced, curriculum-aligned songs" with lyrics, voice and album art "in under a minute" (company claim; MagicSchool, April 2026). MagicSchool has a free-forever plan for teachers. Plus costs $8.33 per user a month billed annually ($99.96 a year) or $12.99 monthly. SSO and SIS and LMS integration (Clever, ClassLink, Canvas, Schoology) come with Enterprise, its custom-priced plan for districts (MagicSchool pricing). The launch post doesn't say which plans include the song tool. Knowt's AI Song Generator has an "Align to standards" field and says it can create "full songs, lyrics, and melodies" (Knowt). Knowt has a free Basic plan, and Ultra costs $12.99 a month billed annually ($149.99) or $24.99 monthly (Knowt plans). So the threat we were watching, a platform with district reach adding audio, has already happened. A teacher can get a sung, aligned song on any topic in about a minute, at no cost, from a tool many already use.

What we didn't find is the rest of the loop. We found no sign that MagicSchool or Knowt puts the song inside a game, checks recall against the lyrics, or reports per-student results on standards-tagged content. Our own custom jams are AI-generated too. Pricing says "our AI generates a complete, curriculum-aligned song in minutes", and our Terms say AI-generated content is reviewed for accuracy and age-appropriateness, but "we cannot guarantee it is error-free". Our public pages don't name the model behind it. So custom jams are now table stakes, not our edge. Our edge is what comes after the song: games played inside the music, a curated library reviewed for accuracy and age, per-student analytics on standards-tagged content, and K-8 vocabulary with grade-level definitions.

District administrators decide, and vendor contracts face cuts.

District administrators, especially curriculum staff, hold most of the buying power. In an EdWeek Research Center survey of district leaders (June to August 2024), 67% said district administrators other than the superintendent have major influence on curriculum purchasing, compared with 56% for teachers (Education Week, February 2025). Privacy and accessibility come first.

For a K-8 product, privacy and accessibility are the entry ticket. The amended COPPA rule took effect on June 23, 2025, with compliance required by April 22, 2026 (Federal Register). It adds separate parental consent for sharing children's data with third parties, a written data-retention policy and a written information security program (Loeb & Loeb; FTC). The FTC chose not to write the school-authorization exception into the rule and will keep relying on its existing guidance (govinfo). That guidance recommends that schools or districts, not individual teachers, decide whether a service's practices are appropriate (FTC COPPA FAQ). This matters for any teacher-led sign-up, including ours. Per our privacy policy, children use JamWise only through teacher-managed classrooms or parent-managed family accounts. Whether one teacher can authorize student accounts for a school is a gray area. That's our reading, and we'll check it with privacy counsel.

State laws add direct duties for vendors. California's SOPIPA bans targeted advertising, profiling and sale of student data (FPF). New York Education Law 2-d requires a Parents' Bill of Rights and a vendor security plan in every contract, plus encryption in transit and at rest (Bond Schoeneck & King; NYSED). Illinois SOPPA requires a written agreement with every operator that receives student data, and districts must post those agreements (CPS; 105 ILCS 85). Many districts use the Student Data Privacy Consortium's agreements. SDPC reports 233,924 active data privacy agreements in its registry, with participating districts in 41 states (SDPC). Common Sense's Privacy Seal, met by fewer than 10% of evaluated companies, is held by Kahoot, Prodigy and Wayground (formerly Quizizz), among others (Common Sense).

Our published policy is a good start. The privacy policy, updated February 20, 2026, includes a COPPA statement. It names Resend (email delivery for waitlist and account emails), Stripe (payments), Cloudflare (content delivery and asset storage) and PostHog (anonymous product analytics). It says we use no Google Analytics or ad tracking, commits to deletion within 30 days of account deletion and lists privacy@jamwise.app as the contact (JamWise privacy policy). The Pricing FAQ adds that we're COPPA compliant by design, don't track children, show no ads and sell no data, and that every jam is reviewed for age-appropriateness (JamWise Pricing). For Parents promises "no tracking" (JamWise For Parents). District reviewers will still want the written security program, retention schedule and signed agreements below to stand behind these statements. They may also ask how "no tracking" fits with PostHog analytics, so a one-line clarification will help.

Accessibility is just as pressing. The Justice Department's ADA Title II rule requires WCAG 2.1 AA for school district web content, including content that contractors provide (ADA.gov). An interim final rule published April 20, 2026 pushed the deadlines back a year, to April 26, 2027 for entities serving 50,000 people or more, and April 26, 2028 for smaller ones and special districts (Federal Register; Tech & Learning). The 2027 date falls inside our likely first district sales window. For us, that means accurate captions or lyric transcripts for all 250+ songs, plus keyboard and screen-reader support in the games. Audio-based games like Lyric Lockdown and Rhythm Recall will need carefully designed alternatives.

Districts also expect rostering. Clever is free for schools, but app partners pay to roster through its Secure Sync service. Songs help recall. That's the honest claim.

The research behind our approach is real, but conditional. Wallace (1994) found that adults recalled text better when it was sung to a simple melody repeated across verses than when it was spoken. When the melody changed from verse to verse, the benefit disappeared (Wallace, 1994). In a small randomized study of 32 children aged 9 to 11, a sung-word group recalled about 20% more words than a spoken group after interference, and about 17% more after a 15-minute delay (Knott & Thaut, 2018). Other studies cut the other way. Kilgour, Jakobson and Cuddy (2000) found that university students recalled sung lyrics better than spoken ones, but the sung advantage disappeared once the spoken version was slowed to match the song's presentation rate (Kilgour et al., 2000). Racette and Peretz (2007) found that singing or speaking the lyrics made no difference to recall, and university students recalled fewer words when singing than when speaking (Racette & Peretz, 2007). A study of younger and older adults found no overall benefit of music for word learning: learners with lower working memory did worse with music, and those with higher working memory did somewhat better (Ong & Chan, 2023). We found no meta-analysis of sung versus spoken recall in children, no rigorous K-8 classroom trial of curriculum songs, and no studies testing whether matching genre to content adds value.

The stronger evidence is for retrieval practice. A systematic review of 50 classroom experiments (49 effect sizes, 5,374 students) found that retrieval practice improved learning compared with non-retrieval conditions such as restudy, and 57% of the effects were medium or large (Agarwal, Nunes & Blunt, 2021). Game-based learning shows modest average effects: about d = 0.29 for learning and 0.36 for retention, larger when games are combined with other instruction and used over several sessions (Wouters et al., 2013), and g = 0.33 for digital games against non-game conditions in K-16 settings (Clark, Tanner-Smith & Killingsworth, 2016). A 2025 meta-analysis of 43 Kahoot studies reported an effect of 0.77 on achievement (Özdemir, 2025). A review of 93 Kahoot studies, co-authored by Kahoot co-founder Alf Inge Wang, concluded that Kahoot can improve learning, while some studies showed little or no effect (Wang & Tahir, 2020; NTNU). Large effects from small, mostly short studies shouldn't be our benchmark. In our judgment, if JamWise works as intended, a realistic expectation is a small to moderate effect on aligned assessments and a smaller one on standardized tests.

Competitors already sell on evidence. Nearpod's ESSA page lists two Level II quasi-experimental studies and one Level III study (company claim; Nearpod ESSA research). Flocabulary commissioned a 2022-23 LearnPlatform study "designed to satisfy Level II requirements" (company claim). The full report is password-protected, so we haven't seen its findings (Renaissance research). ESSA Tier IV ("demonstrates a rationale") needs a rationale based on high-quality research or positive evaluation, usually set out as a logic model, plus ongoing efforts to study effects. It doesn't need a statistically significant result (20 U.S.C. 7801(21); U.S. Department of Education guidance; Kentucky DOE). Tier IV counts as "evidence-based" wherever the law uses the term, except for school-improvement activities funded under ESEA section 1003, which need Tiers I to III (20 U.S.C. 7801(21); California DOE). Other vendors publish Tier IV logic models next to a planned Tier III study (company claim; Derivita). Our analytics can supply usage data for a Tier III study with partner districts. To qualify, it has to be a well-designed correlational study with statistical controls for selection bias, showing a statistically significant positive effect on a relevant outcome. Until then, the honest line is that songs can help students remember facts and vocabulary, not that songs improve learning in general.

What makes us different: the loop, accuracy and child safety.

Our clearest difference isn't one feature. It's the loop. A short song introduces the content. A game like Lyric Lockdown or Rhythm Recall uses the same audio to make students recall it. Real-time analytics show how each student is doing on standards-tagged content. That matches what the research favors: repeated retrieval, combined with instruction, over several sessions. It also fills the gaps in the alternatives. Flocabulary's practice activities (Vocab Cards, Vocab Game, Read & Respond, Quiz) are text-based, and Lyric Lab is rhyme writing over a beat, not a game played against the original song (Flocabulary, How it works). Quiz platforms have games without content. General AI song tools have content without accuracy checks, alignment or under-13 access. Education song generators like MagicSchool's and Knowt's now add alignment, but we found no games played against the song and no per-student data.

Here's the honest version. Since April 2026, a song made to order isn't ours alone. MagicSchool makes one in under a minute from a topic and grade level, on a free plan. So we won't sell custom jams as the reason to pay. We sell four things together: games played inside the music, a curated library reviewed for accuracy and age, per-student analytics on standards-tagged content, and K-8 vocabulary, with 1,801 glossary terms defined by grade (JamWise Glossary). Any one of them can be matched. Together, today, we didn't find them anywhere else.

Our second edge is trust. A curated library can promise accurate lyrics, age-appropriate language, a consistent format and clear licensing. General AI tools can't. That matters more as districts tighten privacy review and the Suno lawsuits continue. Our Pricing FAQ says every jam is reviewed for age-appropriateness, and our Terms say AI-generated content is reviewed for accuracy but can't be guaranteed error-free. So the trust edge depends on that review being visible, and applied equally to the library and to AI custom jams. Our third edge is access. Teachers start free, with no credit card, on a plan that includes the full standards-aligned library and basic analytics. Flocabulary limits that to a trial. MagicSchool is free to start too, so access helps, but it won't win on its own.

Part of this rests on what we didn't find. We didn't test competitor products while logged in, so we'll double-check the claim that no one else offers audio-based games before it goes into marketing.

We don't need to out-catalog Flocabulary, out-price Gimkit or out-generate Suno and MagicSchool.
