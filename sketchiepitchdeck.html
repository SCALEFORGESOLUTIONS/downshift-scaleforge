The moment

You publish it. Then someone says the second sentence is wrong.

On a tool that renders in one shot, that sentence costs a whole new generation. The rest of the video comes back different too: new drawings, new timing, everything to re-check. So the correction gets skipped, the video goes quietly out of date, or the video never gets made.

The metric that decides whether an explainer is worth making is not how fast the first render lands. It is how long the whole loop takes — draft, watch, notice, fix, ship. Almost nobody prices that loop. Sketchie is built around it.

The architecture, live

A video compiled from a document you can write back to.

Click a scene to watch it draw.

An illustration of the model, drawn in SVG for this deck. Not a screenshot and not Sketchie output; timing is compressed. Real renders: sketchie.ai/examples.

Every generation returns two artifacts: the MP4, and the scene graph it was compiled from. Scenes carry a heading, their narration, and elements bound by revealWord to the exact spoken word that reveals them.

Because the graph exists before anything is spent on voice or rendering, quality can be checked first — and because scenes are addressable, a correction touches one of them.

schema published under MIT

The wedge

Fix one scene. Nothing else moves.

Two ways to edit

Change the heading, narration, labels or icon styling directly. Or describe the change in plain language, in the app or as a single instruction to the edit endpoint.

Versions, not overwrites

A re-render appends a version to the same explainer. A revert endpoint points the head back at an earlier ready version. History stays intact.

The first edit is free

Editing never consumes one of the two free videos, and each video's first re-render costs nothing. Later ones draw seconds of minutes rather than a whole new video.

This is the architecture, not a feature bolted on afterwards. A competitor can render faster; making a finished one-shot file retroactively addressable is a different product.

Eleven design laws, each with an effect size and a link to its primary source. The grades are the point: a working assumption is labelled as one rather than dressed as a finding.

Not a manifesto, a gate

A graph that breaks the spec never becomes a video.

Layer A runs mechanical assertions on the scene graph at generation time, before a cent goes on voice or rendering: duration ceiling, per-scene band, element count, reveal words that resolve, the post-scene silence, the question-and-recap ending. A failing graph is regenerated with its own failures fed back into the prompt.

That layer is open source under MIT, with a runnable checker and a worked passing example. Layers B (model-as-judge) and C (checks on the rendered file) are described but not published.

What is actually watchable today

Nineteen finished renders, 0:33 to 5:56.

Range, not a highlight reel

Concepts from compound interest to neural networks, at eleven lengths climbing to the six-minute ceiling. The same explainer is also published in two alternate layouts.

Document mode

Raw meeting notes rendered as a 5:02 walkthrough, and a long written explanation turned into a 4:36 lesson. It follows the source instead of writing over it.

One prompt, several languages

The same sky-blue explainer generated natively in Spanish, Brazilian Portuguese and German — script, on-screen labels and narration, in one voice.

Six narrators — Nora, Miles, Dean, Clara, Piper, Wren — previewable before you generate, each able to speak every supported language, so a course keeps one consistent voice.

The developer surface

One POST in. MP4 and scene graph out.

Creation is asynchronous — 202 and a queued record, then poll or hold a Server-Sent Events stream and catch each status transition. The edit endpoint turns one plain-language instruction into a targeted re-render; revert points the head back.

Generation endpoints allow 20 requests a minute, everything else shares 200. There is an MCP server with five tools, running as a local stdio process for Claude Code and Claude Desktop. A hosted connector for Claude web and mobile is on the roadmap.

Say this before they find it

Where we are behind, today.

Raw speed

Sketchie's own comparison page concedes that Simi is faster at first generation. The counter-argument is the whole loop, not the first render — and it has to be demonstrated, not asserted.

Single-user

There is no shared workspace today. Shared workspaces are on the roadmap. Do not sell team collaboration.

No proof but the output

No customers, counts, testimonials or press are published, and the company states it launched in July 2026 with no venture funding. The nineteen renders and the open eval repo are the evidence that exists.

Conceding the metric you lose on is the most efficient way to be believed on the one you win.

The ask

Make two videos. Break one on purpose. Fix it.

Two videos free, up to a minute each, watermarked, no card, full product including the scene editor. The loop is the thing to judge, so judge the loop.
